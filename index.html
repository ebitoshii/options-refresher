Options — Master Reference

Complete theory, strategies, and institutional applications for professional derivatives traders.

What Is an Option?

An option is a contractual right — not an obligation — to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specified strike price on or before a stated expiry. The buyer pays a premium; the seller collects it and assumes the obligation.

CALL OPTION
Right to Buy

Grants the holder the right to purchase the underlying at the strike price. Value rises as the underlying appreciates. Max loss = premium paid.

PUT OPTION
Right to Sell

Grants the holder the right to sell the underlying at the strike price. Value rises as the underlying depreciates. Max loss = premium paid.

AMERICAN
Exercisable Anytime

Can be exercised at any point before or on expiry. Commands a higher premium than European. Equities and ETFs are typically American style.

EUROPEAN
Exercisable at Expiry

Exercise is only possible at expiry. Indices (SPX, VIX) are typically European. Allows cleaner pricing via Black-Scholes.

Option Premium Decomposition

Premium = Intrinsic Value + Time Value
Intrinsic Value (Call) = max(S − K, 0)
Intrinsic Value (Put) = max(K − S, 0)

Time value decays non-linearly — it accelerates as expiry approaches (theta decay). ATM options have the highest time value. Deep ITM and deep OTM options have minimal time value.

Black-Scholes Framework

The Black-Scholes model prices European options under five assumptions: log-normally distributed returns, constant volatility, no dividends, risk-free rate constant, frictionless markets.

C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂)
P = K·e^(−rT)·N(−d₂) − S·N(−d₁)
d₁ = [ln(S/K) + (r + σ²/2)T] / (σ√T)   |   d₂ = d₁ − σ√T

Where S = spot, K = strike, r = risk-free rate, T = time to expiry, σ = implied volatility, N(·) = cumulative normal distribution.

Put-Call Parity

Foundational no-arbitrage relationship for European options:

C − P = S − K·e^(−rT)

A violation creates a risk-free arbitrage. Institutions use this to synthesise positions and spot mispricing across legs.

The Greeks

Greeks measure sensitivities of option value to changes in underlying parameters. Institutional desks manage Greeks in aggregate across their book — not position-by-position.

Δ
Delta
Rate of change of option price vs. underlying move. Range: −1 to +1.
Γ
Gamma
Rate of change of delta. Highest ATM and near expiry. Long gamma = convexity.
Θ
Theta
Daily time decay. Option sellers collect theta. Negative for long options.
V
Vega
Sensitivity to 1% change in implied vol. Long options are long vega.
ρ
Rho
Sensitivity to interest rate changes. More significant for long-dated options.

Delta (Δ) — Directional Exposure

Delta approximates the equivalent share exposure of an options position. A call with Δ = 0.50 behaves like holding 50 shares per 100-share contract. Institutions aggregate delta across their entire portfolio to understand net directional exposure.

Long Call: Δ ∈ (0, 1)   |   Long Put: Δ ∈ (−1, 0)   |   Short Call: Δ ∈ (−1, 0)   |   Short Put: Δ ∈ (0, 1)
Institutional Use — Delta Hedging

A market-maker who sells 1,000 SPX calls with Δ = 0.45 is short −450 deltas. To hedge, they buy 450 "delta-equivalent" units of SPX futures. As the market moves, they continuously rebalance — this dynamic delta hedging is a core desk function.

Gamma (Γ) — Convexity

Gamma measures how quickly delta changes as the underlying moves. Long gamma positions (long options) benefit from large moves in either direction — the delta "self-adjusts" in your favour. Short gamma positions (sold options) are hurt by large moves.

PositionGammaP&L ProfileBreakeven Condition
Long calls / putsLong ΓProfits from large movesMove must exceed theta decay
Short calls / putsShort ΓProfits from small moves / stagnationUnderlying stays near strike
Gamma Scalping

An institution buys ATM straddles (long gamma) and repeatedly delta-hedges as the underlying oscillates. Each hedge locks in a small P&L. If realised volatility exceeds implied volatility, the cumulative scalping profit exceeds the premium paid — a core vol arbitrage strategy.

Theta (Θ) — Time Decay

Theta represents the daily erosion of an option's value, all else equal. It is negative for option buyers and positive for option sellers. Theta is not linear — it accelerates in the final 30 days before expiry, particularly for ATM options.

Θ = −(S·σ·N'(d₁)) / (2√T) − r·K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂)   [for calls]

Vega (ν) — Volatility Sensitivity

Vega measures the dollar change in option value per 1% rise in implied volatility. Both calls and puts are long vega when bought. Vega is highest for ATM options and longer-dated options. Institutions with large options books actively manage vega exposure — a sudden vol spike (e.g., VIX move from 16 to 35) can severely impact a short-vega book.

ν = S · √T · N'(d₁)

Options Strategies

Each strategy encodes a view on direction, volatility, and time. Institutions choose legs based on cost efficiency, regulatory capital treatment, and risk profile.

Single-Leg Positions

BULLISH LONG CALL
Long Call

Max loss: premium. Max gain: unlimited. Use: leveraged bullish bet, defined-risk substitute for long stock.

BEARISH LONG PUT
Long Put

Max loss: premium. Max gain: strike − premium. Use: directional short, portfolio hedge.

YIELD SHORT CALL
Short Call (Naked)

Max gain: premium. Max loss: unlimited. Dangerous without hedge. Used by desks only with offsetting inventory.

YIELD SHORT PUT
Short Put (Cash-Secured)

Max gain: premium. Max loss: strike − premium. Use: get paid to acquire stock at desired entry level.

Spreads — Defined Risk, Defined Reward

Bull Call Spread
Buy K1 Call | Sell K2 Call (K2 > K1)
Reduces cost of a long call by selling upside. Max gain = K2 − K1 − net debit. Max loss = net debit. Use when moderately bullish — best in low-vol environments.
Bear Put Spread
Buy K1 Put | Sell K2 Put (K2 < K1)
Reduces cost of a long put by selling downside. Max gain = K1 − K2 − net debit. Use when moderately bearish and vol is expensive.
Bull Put Spread
Sell K1 Put | Buy K2 Put (K2 < K1)
Credit received upfront. Max gain = net credit. Max loss = K1 − K2 − credit. Use: bullish / neutral, generates income, defined downside risk.
Bear Call Spread
Sell K1 Call | Buy K2 Call (K2 > K1)
Credit received. Bearish income trade. Max loss capped at spread width minus credit. Use: range-bound or mildly bearish views.

Volatility Strategies

Long Straddle
Buy ATM Call | Buy ATM Put (same expiry, same strike)
Long gamma, long vega. Profits if underlying moves significantly in either direction. Loses to time decay. Use: pre-event trades (earnings, FOMC), when expecting vol expansion.
Short Straddle
Sell ATM Call | Sell ATM Put
Short gamma, short vega. Maximum yield if spot stays pinned near strike at expiry. Risk: unlimited on both sides. Requires active delta hedging. Classic MM strategy.
Long Strangle
Buy OTM Call | Buy OTM Put
Cheaper than straddle but needs a larger move to profit. Same long-vol theme. Better risk-adjusted for large tail scenarios (e.g., binary events).
Short Strangle
Sell OTM Call | Sell OTM Put
Wider profit zone than short straddle. Collects premium as long as underlying stays within a range. A core hedge-fund theta-harvesting approach.
Iron Condor
Bull Put Spread + Bear Call Spread
Four-leg. Profit: spot stays within inner strikes. All risk defined. Popular for systematic income in institutional vol books. No naked exposure.
Long Butterfly
Buy K1, Sell 2×K2, Buy K3 Call
Max profit at K2 at expiry. Net debit, low cost. Targets a very specific spot outcome. Used by institutions to express pin risk around a level.

Institutional Applications

Institutions use options not just directionally but as precision tools for hedging, yield enhancement, regulatory capital management, and structured product construction.

1. Portfolio Hedging

Index Put Buying (Tail Hedge)

A long-only equity fund manager buys OTM SPX puts to protect against a sharp drawdown. The puts act as portfolio insurance — they appreciate dramatically if markets sell off, partially or fully offsetting equity losses.

Scenario: Pension Fund Tail Protection

A $5B equity fund buys 3-month SPX puts at 5% OTM, spending ~0.5% of NAV in premium. If SPX drops 20%, put payoff may offset 8–12% of losses. The cost is a drag on returns but reduces max drawdown risk — critical for defined-benefit liability matching.

Collar — Zero-Cost Hedge

Collar = Long Stock + Long OTM Put + Short OTM Call

The sold call premium finances the put. Net cost approaches zero. Upside is capped at the call strike; downside is protected below the put strike. Used extensively by corporate insiders and concentrated-equity holders.

Protective Put on Single Name

Portfolio managers hedging concentrated single-stock risk. More expensive than index puts due to correlation risk — individual names carry idiosyncratic risk not present in the index.

2. Covered Call Writing (Yield Enhancement)

Covered Call = Long Underlying + Short OTM Call

An institution holding a large equity position sells OTM calls to generate premium income. Works well in flat-to-mildly-bullish markets. The premium received reduces the cost basis of the holding. Risk: stock rallies above the strike and position gets called away (opportunity cost).

Scenario: Equity Income Fund

A UK equity income fund holds £500M of FTSE 100 stocks. Each month, the desk sells 1-month calls at 2–3% OTM, collecting ~0.3–0.5% per month in premium. Annualised, this generates ~4–6% extra income — boosting total return in sideways markets.

3. Cash-Secured Put Selling (Stock Acquisition)

An institution wants to acquire stock at a lower price. Instead of placing a limit order, they sell ATM or OTM puts. If assigned, they buy the stock at the strike (effectively at a discount = strike − premium). If not assigned, they keep the premium. A disciplined entry strategy.

4. Volatility Arbitrage

The desk trades the spread between implied volatility (what the market prices into options) and realised volatility (what the market actually does). If implied vol is elevated relative to realised vol, a vol seller will profit over time.

Expected P&L ≈ ½ · Γ · S² · (σ²_realised − σ²_implied)
Scenario: Delta-Hedged Straddle Sale

The desk sells an ATM SPX straddle when 30-day IV = 22%. The realised volatility over the next 30 days turns out to be 15%. With continuous delta hedging, the desk captures the vol spread as gamma-scalping profit — a positive carry position.

5. Dispersion Trading

A sophisticated institutional strategy: sell index vol (short straddle on SPX) and buy single-stock vol (long straddles on SPX components). Profits when single-stock correlation is lower than the index implies — i.e., stocks move independently rather than together.

Dispersion P&L ∝ (Implied Correlation) − (Realised Correlation)

6. Risk Reversal

Risk Reversal = Long OTM Call + Short OTM Put (or vice versa)

Near-zero-cost directional bet. FX desks use risk reversals extensively. In equities, selling OTM puts and buying OTM calls generates net credit while expressing a bullish view. The put skew typically makes the OTM put more expensive, so the sold put generates higher premium.

8. Options Overlay Programmes

Large pension funds and endowments run systematic options overlay programmes — selling covered calls or cash-secured puts across their entire equity book on a rolling basis. Managed by a dedicated options desk or outsourced to an options overlay manager. Target: 1–3% annual premium income to supplement equity returns.

9. Regulatory & Capital Considerations

Under Basel III / FRTB, options positions generate capital charges based on delta, vega, and curvature sensitivities. Institutions optimise their book to minimise RWA (Risk-Weighted Assets) while maintaining desired P&L exposure. Defined-risk strategies (spreads, collars) attract lower capital charges than naked short options.

Volatility — The Core Variable

For institutions, vol is the primary tradeable quantity. Direction is secondary to understanding and trading the vol surface.

The Volatility Surface

A 3D map of implied volatility across strikes (moneyness) and expiries (term structure). The surface is not flat — it has two key features:

Volatility Skew (Smile)

In equity markets, OTM puts carry higher IV than OTM calls — the "put skew." This reflects demand for downside protection and the empirical observation that markets fall faster than they rise. The skew steepens in risk-off environments.

Skew = IV(95% strike) − IV(105% strike)   [typically positive for equities]

Term Structure

IV varies across expiries. In normal conditions, longer-dated options have slightly higher IV (upward sloping). Before risk events (earnings, elections, FOMC), the near-term IV spikes above the long-term — creating an inverted (backwardated) term structure.

VIX — The Fear Gauge

The CBOE VIX measures 30-day implied volatility of the S&P 500 using a model-free replication of variance across the option chain. It is not simply the ATM vol — it integrates the full skew.

VIX² = (2/T) · Σ [ΔK/K²] · e^(rT) · Q(K) − (1/T) · [F/K₀ − 1]²
VIX LevelMarket RegimeInstitutional Response
< 15Extreme complacency / low vol regimeBuy vol cheaply — long straddles, long puts as hedge
15–20Normal market conditionsNeutral vol positioning, systematic selling in range
20–30Elevated uncertaintyReduce short gamma exposure; increase hedge ratio
> 30Distress / crisisBuy back short vol, sell puts into the spike if cash-rich

Vol Regime & Strategy Selection

LOW VOL
Buy Vol

Long straddles, long strangles, long variance. Cheap options — you pay little for large move potential. Best before known catalysts.

HIGH VOL
Sell Vol

Iron condors, short strangles, covered calls. Premium is rich — harvest theta. Requires active delta hedging and risk management.

VOL SPIKE
Mean Reversion

Sell near-term vol, buy far-term. Calendar spreads. Volatility spikes are statistically mean-reverting — sell the panic.

SKEW TRADE
Risk Reversal

Trade the skew differential: buy cheap OTM calls, sell expensive OTM puts (or vice versa). Pure skew exposure.
